Supplementary Notes A) Description of beads and Martini 3 model


A1) Blocks, chemical types and sizes
       Martini 3 is composed of seven chemical bead types: polar (P), intermediate/non-
polar (N), apolar (C), halo-compounds (X), monovalent ions (Q), divalent ions (D) and
water (W). All of these bead types come in three different sizes: regular (R-bead, assumed
default), small (S-bead, denoted with prefix S), and tiny (T-bead, denoted with prefix T).
X, D, and W are completely new chemical types that did not exist in the previous version.
Except for W and D beads, all beads have subtypes that are distinguished by a number
indicating the relative degree of polarity (from 1, low polarity, to a maximum of 6, high
polarity). These subtypes allow for a more accurate representation of the chemical nature
of the underlying atomistic structure. Other chemically-specific subtypes are described in
section A4. In comparison with the previous version, N and Q beads underwent the
largest expansion, to increase coverage of the chemical space. From the oil-water
partition perspective, N-beads involve most of the compounds1, which naturally demands
for more options to allow for the development of accurate models.
       The chemical bead types are separated in blocks, each with independent sets of
parameters. P-, N-, C-, and X-beads are part of the organic block while Q- and D-beads
are part of the ion block. For now, W is the only bead that is part of the solvent block.
Each block was parametrized to follow certain trends that depend on chemical types and
sizes. Here is a short description of the properties of the organic and the ion blocks. For
a more elaborate description of the parametrization strategy, including the solvent block,
see sections B2-B4. Supplementary Figure 1 summarizes the key trends of chemical
types and sizes while quantitative data about the beads is presented in sections A5-A6.

Organic block: the self-interaction and the interaction with water beads are gradually
reduced, as the beads of the same size are more hydrophobic (P > N > C, see
Supplementary Figure 1A). As the difference in hydrophobicity increases, the beads
also tend to form heterogeneous biphasic systems. In organic molecules, an important
exception for these trends are halo-compounds (Supplementary Figure 1B). In their
case, as the molecules are more hydrophobic, the self-interaction actually increases. This
is the reason why these compounds have a dedicated chemical type (X-beads) in Martini

                                                                                         4
3. The trends of the organic block are also different regarding size compared to the
previous version of Martini. Smaller beads represent organic fragments with fewer carbon
atoms, which reduces their self-interaction and their interaction with the environment but
makes them more hydrophilic2.

Ion block: the key difference for the trends involving ions in Martini are related with
changes in properties of different sizes (see Supplementary Figure 1C). Contrary to the
organic block, smaller Q/D beads have stronger interactions due to stronger electrostatic
interactions. At the same time, the smaller sizes increase their interactions with the
environment, in particular with water and polar solvents, which is also opposite compared
to the organic block.

A2) Interaction matrix
         The interaction matrix (Supplementary Table 1) is the core of the Martini force
field, with all the assumptions for the behavior of the different chemical types encoded in
the choice of the interaction levels. As in Martini 2, a limited number of 𝜀𝑖𝑗 and 𝜎𝑖𝑗 values
is used; such values are classified as different levels of attractive, intermediate, and
repulsive interactions. In fact, all interactions are attractive, and this naming is only used
to indicate the net effect of the interaction balance between the pair of beads. The number
of the levels is indicative of these trends, with lower numbers representing higher 𝜀𝑖𝑗
values.
         More levels are now defined in Martini 3, which allows a smoother transition
between the different chemical bead types. For the organic and ion-organic block
interactions, a total of 22 levels are defined now, while Martini 2 had only 10 levels for
these interactions. In addition, W, D, and Q beads have their own set of levels in Martini
3. Although different size combinations have different definitions for the interaction levels,
they all follow the same trend defined by the interaction matrix, which is an assumption
of the model. The interaction matrix is symmetric regarding the default LJ parameters
used. However, because of the p-factor approach (see section B3), the interactions
between ions and neutral molecules are not symmetric for cross-interactions of different
sizes.

A4) Labels
       Specific subtypes (generically called “labels” here) are an easy way to fine-tune
the CG models in Martini 3. The concept already existed in the previous versions but was
only applied to N and Q types to take into account different hydrogen bond capabilities in
molecules with a similar degree of hydrophobicity. In Martini 3, labels were expanded and
can be applied to all beads, slightly changing their behaviors, which is translated into
small changes in the interaction matrix. Three of these labels (hydrogen bonding, electron
polarizability, positive/negative) are chemically specific, which means they are only
applied to certain chemical types. The other two labels (self-interaction, partial charge)
are more generic, and can be applied on top of all beads of the organic block, including
beads which have already been modified with some of the chemically specific labels. A
detailed description of each type of label is given below.

Supplementary Notes B) Parametrization strategy of the beads

       First of all, the reparametrization strategy of a complete CG force field like Martini
needs to define two key aspects of the new model: (i) which potentials will be used; and
(ii) which properties and systems will be considered for the calibration and validation. Both
aspects are linked, as the potentials will limit the properties which can be quantitatively
obtained. In Martini 3, the functional forms for bonded (bonds, angles, and dihedrals) and
non-bonded interactions (Lennard-Jones (LJ) and Coulombic) are the same as in
previous versions. For non-bonded interactions (Equation 1), a cutoff of 1.1 nm is the
default choice, as defined by de Jong et al.8 Although the LJ potential is not the most
accurate possibility for CG models9,10, this option allows Martini to be easily implemented
in atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulation programs. In this way, Martini can
benefit from decades of development in MD codes like GROMACS11,12, including their
fast highly-parallel performances and their efficient implementations of modern enhanced
sampling techniques. The choice of the LJ potential is therefore justified by the speed of
computation, that is the main interest of a CG force field.

       The main disadvantage of a hard potential such as the LJ is the limited range of
the liquid phase9, which implies that the overall interaction strength of Martini needs to be
underestimated in relation to atomistic models, otherwise single beads could easily end
up in the solid phase. This problem was already observed for certain systems with Martini
2 water, which was avoided by the non-optimal use of antifreeze waters13,14. Only certain
very polar compounds (as primary amides) and ionic salts formed by single beads should
be solid at room temperature (around 298 to 310 K). As a consequence of the use of the
LJ potential, some properties, such as free energies of solvation, free energies of
vaporization, or gas-liquid surface tension, cannot be used as references for the
parametrization (although good trends are desirable). On the other hand, properties that
involve ΔΔG measurements, like the oil/water transfer free energy or the excess free
energy of mixing, can still be quantitatively obtained by such CG models. As partitioning
                                                                                                    19
and miscibility are important to reproduce a wide range of phenomena, they were chosen
to be the main targets for the parametrization of bead-bead interactions in Martini 3.
       One aspect that differentiates Martini from other CG force fields, and that needed
to be considered in the reparametrization as well, is the generic building block approach.
The Martini model contains generic beads that can represent different chemical moieties.
They are classified in chemical and size classes that follow general trends in
hydrophobicity, miscibility, and self-interaction. The key advantage of this approach is the
transferability of the model, which makes Martini very versatile and user-friendly. The
main disadvantage is that the quality of the models does not only depend on the optimized
LJ parameters, but also on four other interdependent ingredients of the model: atom-to-
bead mapping of the molecules, bonded parameters, number of bead types available,
and bead type assignment. The impact of LJ parameters and bonded parameter choices,
in terms of bead sizes and bond distances, was recently discussed by Alessandri et al.2
Mapping, number of bead types, and bead type assignment were also recently explored
by Kanekal and Bereau1.
       With so many variables, problems such as too strong protein-protein
interactions15,16 are very hard to pinpoint and cannot be fully solved with one simple
modification. All aspects of the model need to be reviewed, with an iterative
parametrization strategy starting from the LJ parameters of single beads and ending with
validation in complex target systems, such as lipid bilayers, proteins, or polymers. In the
next sections, we describe all essential aspects of the new parametrization. As already
mentioned in section A1, the beads are now divided in 3 blocks: organic, ion, and solvent.
Each block has its unique set of interaction levels, including different parameters and
approaches for the self and cross-interactions between bead sizes. This choice facilitates
the implementation of future improvements in the model, as the blocks of parameters are
largely independent of one another. For instance, future improvements in the polarizable
water5,6 based on Martini 3 will mainly require changes in water (solvent block) and
charged beads (ion block).
       The next sections (B1 to B4) describe how we defined the bead sizes, masses
and interaction strengths, including self-interactions (R-R, S-S, and T-T) and cross-
interactions (R-S, R-T, and S-T).

B2) Organic block
       The organic block makes up most of the beads used in Martini 3. They can model
organic molecules, or fragments of them, mainly consisting of atoms of the 2nd period of
the periodic table. Sulfur, phosphorus and halogen atoms are also included. The main
idea here was initially to balance the interaction levels used between all bead sizes as
well as possible for Martini models to be used in multiresolution way, with further
optimization at the end towards the experimental calibration targets. The parametrization
workflow is represented in Supplementary Figure 3.
       The step 1 was to build prototype models of polar and nonpolar molecules in all
three possible resolutions. For example, prototype models of dodecane were generated
considering models with three R-beads, four S-beads, and six T-beads (see Figure 1A).
Following the building block approach, the same beads need to be used in apolar
fragments that are part of amphiphilic polar molecules, such as alcohols with a long
aliphatic chain. From this point, the step 2 was to explore the LJ parameter space, with
initial predefined sizes (𝜎𝑖𝑗 , discussed in the previous section) and variable interaction
strengths (𝜀𝑖𝑗 ). We built simulation boxes containing one solute molecule immersed in a
solvent, with all combinations of different sizes. MD simulations were performed in the

molecules mapped only with regular beads and using interactions from Martini 2 (𝜀𝑅𝑅(2.0) ).

       Steps 1 to 3 were performed to guarantee that the parameters obtained are
balanced across the different sizes. It is important to notice that Martini 3 is based on the
use of specific pair interactions for 𝜀𝑖𝑗 and not on combination rules. The whole procedure
until here only provided a few interaction levels used to define the pair interactions. It is
important to use enough prototype models that allow the procedure to generate 𝜀𝑖𝑗 values
for points near to the extremes of the interaction matrix. The rest of the parameters are
defined by interpolation and (if necessary) extrapolation, forming the interaction levels of
Martini. The procedure described until step 3 naturally leads to reasonable trends in self-
interaction and hydrophobicity for beads of different sizes that represent the same
chemical fragment at different resolutions (like propylamine, ethylamine, and
methylamine). Smaller beads are more hydrophilic and interact less with the environment
(and with themselves). This behavior mimics the expected trends observed in organic
molecules with different number of aliphatic carbons2. Trends of Martini 3 beads in
different sizes and chemical types were described in section A1.

B3) Ion block
        The main difference between neutral and charged (Q and D) beads in Martini is
that the latter ones include not only LJ but also Coulomb interactions. These electrostatic


                                                                                                        26
interactions take into account a default explicit screening, with a relative permittivity
𝜖𝑟 =15. The parametrization of ions in CG force fields is per se challenging, which makes
the definition of the “best” strategy very hard. The main reasons for these difficulties are:

   a) Limited amount of available experimental reference data. Moreover, for very
       hydrophilic ions, partitioning data (logP) are also not very reliable, as it is difficult
       to experimentally measure their concentration in the hydrophobic phase. For
       instance, octanol/water partitioning measurements are usually limited to
       hydrophilic molecules with a minimum logP of -2.521.
   b) For cases where reliable experimental data is available, the partitioning data
       usually reflects the behavior of ion pairs (cation and anion), which are difficult to
       decouple22,23.
   c) Co-existence of different protonation states can be important for charged organic
       molecules. In water, the degree of protonation is determined by easily measurable
       pKa and pH values; but in other environments, pKa values depend on the hydration
       level of the organic molecule, its polarity, and the availability of protons.
   d) Atomistic models are still not very accurate for ions24,25. Currently, most atomistic
       force field developers are trying to improve the behavior of ions by adding non-
       bonded fixes to the LJ pair interactions 26,       rescaling charges27–29 or adding
       polarization explicitly30, but there is still no standardized method applicable to any
       kind of system.
   e) Solubility and osmotic coefficients29,31 can be used to estimate the self-interaction
       of ionic species, but they are mainly available for charged molecules in water and
       not in other solvents.
   f) The chemical diversity of relevant ions is higher than in the organic block. For
       instance, biologically relevant ions can be inorganic (e.g. metals, halides, or
       phosphates) or organic (such as trimethyl ammonium or acetate). This aspect
       makes ions hard to be grouped in generic beads, including difficulties in predicting
       the expected changes with size.

       Given all these problems, the previous version of the Martini model opted for a
simple strategy, which was to only include differences in hydrogen bonding capabilities

                                                                                             27
of ions. The interaction levels were based on the LJ parameters used for the neutral
beads. Besides, the ions were mainly modeled by regular size beads. For small ions,
such as sodium or chloride, this choice meant that the ions somehow include their
hydration shells in the model, which limits their application.
       The Martini 3 parametrization strategy of ions differs from the one used in Martini
2 in several aspects. First of all, as already mentioned, in Martini 3 the interaction levels
of charged beads (ion block) are different from the neutral ones. Moreover, Q-Q self-
interactions take now into account the effective position of the minimum in the total
potential which in this case is the sum of Coulomb and LJ interactions. The LJ 𝜎𝑖𝑗 is
calibrated to keep similar effective bead sizes as defined for the neutral ones. In particular,
for small and tiny beads, the 𝜎𝑖𝑗 usually needs some adjustments to compensate for the
stronger electrostatic interactions between opposite charges which would otherwise allow
too much spatial overlap of the beads.

beads. As neutral beads do not have partial charges in standard (i.e. non-polarizable)

similar reasons, interactions between D- and Q-beads are also not symmetric for the
cross-interactions of different sizes. The chemical types of Q- and D-beads and their
trends are discussed in section B1.


B4) Solvent block
       The solvent block represents a class of models in which more than one molecule
is grouped into a single bead. This could be especially important for solvents consisting
of very small molecules like water, methanol, or ammonia. In these cases, the
representation of each molecule by a single bead would negate the advantage of using a
CG force field. Besides, given the peculiar situation of grouping molecules, the properties
for calibration, such as transfer free energies and miscibility, need to be carefully defined
by clever choices and not only by experimental references. For instance, oil/water transfer
free energy of solvents beads were compared in relation to one water molecule, as it is
not easy to define a good experimental or theoretical reference for dynamic clusters of
molecules. On the other hand, as miscibility depends on the collective interactions
between the molecules that compose the mixture, the number of molecules represented
by the bead needs to be considered for this validation. For instance, a 50:50 (molar
fraction) binary mixture of regular water bead (which represents 4 molecules of water)
and benzene Martini CG model (1 molecule) should actually use a ratio 20:80.
       Currently, the only bead parametrized in the solvent block is water (called “W”).
Given its importance, this W bead has a specific parametrization. With respect to Martini
2, the bead has a reduced self-interaction which avoids the necessity of anti-freeze
waters. The interactions with other beads from the organic block were also adapted to

                                                                                          32
recover the solvation free energies of the water model from Martini 2 (using Equation 3).
The softness parameter of water in relation to the organic and ion blocks (𝑠𝑊𝑗 ) is 0.02 nm
(to be compared to the standard 𝑠𝑖𝑗 = 0.01 nm), which mimics the softer character of a
group of water molecules10. Finally, as for all the other blocks, we also generated smaller
beads for water (called SW and TW, representing three and two water molecules
respectively).
       The interactions between water beads of different sizes were tuned to have an
ideal miscibility (see Figure 1B). Besides, the solvation free energies of the organic block
beads were tuned to be as similar as possible in all water resolutions. SW and TW mix
nearly ideally with RW and with themselves. They are useful for special situations, where
hydration of small cavities (like a ligand binding pocket in a protein) is necessary.


B5) Parameter calibration, tests and validation
       As Martini is based on pair interactions, it is hard to find simple systems for
parameter calibration that cover enough points in the interaction matrix for all bead size
combinations (which correspond to 326,028 pair interactions in Martini 3). So, complex
systems are not only used for validation but are part of the calibration as well. In Martini
2, the calibration of the LJ parameters mainly focused on oil/water transfer free energies
of single beads, with a limited number of solvents fully tested 13. These solvents were
water, hexadecane, chloroform, ether, and octanol. For certain solvents, interfacial
tension and densities were also checked. Regular Q-beads were mainly parametrized to
improve lipid bilayer properties, which were extensively checked using different structural
properties. Lipid simulations also helped to fine-tune the choices of levels in the
interaction matrix. Although a few additional beads were further added with the
parametrization of new biomolecular topologies17,18,34–36, the majority of beads stayed
unchanged since the calibration of the parameters in 2007.
       In Martini 3, the overall approach was also not based on a rigorous separation of
calibration and validation groups. However, there are some differences. First, we
expanded the calibration and validation targets. Second, the protocol was separated in
“tiers”, which represent groups of tests with different level of complexity. Every time
parameters failed to reasonably reproduce the reference data in advanced tiers, the

                                                                                         33
choices of mapping, bead assignment, and bonded parameters were reviewed. If, after
exploring the possible options, the problems persisted, the procedure went back to the
first tier (called “0”), where the specific parameters in the interaction matrix and/or labels
were retuned. In the early iterations, even new bead chemical types and labels were
generated. This iterative process was repeated until a satisfactory agreement between
the simulated properties and the target data was achieved across the whole range of
tested systems. Each tier is described below, with most of the results obtained distributed
in the main text and in sections E and F of this Supporting Information.

Tier 0: Tests performed with single beads, small molecules, and solvents. The goal was
to have the beads following the expected trends and compare simple small-molecule
models with experimental data. So, this tier was mainly used for calibration of LJ
parameters. The tests performed include the computation of free energies (solvation,
hydration, transfer) and miscibility tests. In case of Q particles, trends were compared to
available experimental osmotic coefficient values29.

Tier 1: In this intermediate step, initial tests with bilayers and proteins were performed.
So, Martini 3 prototype models for these biomolecules were already necessary at this tier.
In addition, benchmarks including other important applications were included. Most of the
tests were designed as “yes-or-no” questions to evaluate the overall quality of the force
field. These “quality control tests” included negative and positive controls for protein-
protein and sugar-sugar interactions, as well as checking the performance of reproducing
bilayer structural properties for common lipid types. Points in the interaction levels and
matrix not covered by tier 0 were also tested and fine-tuned.


Tier 2: Quantitative tests involving complex systems, e.g. aedamers, ionic-liquids, phase
coexistence in ternary lipid membranes, dimerization PMFs of membrane proteins, etc.
Usually performed at the end of the parametrization workflow, including quantitative
comparisons with experimental or atomistic simulation data. Here most of the tests are
considered validation, but still with a few refinements in the interaction matrix and
interaction levels.

Supplementary Notes C) Concepts and rules for parametrization of Martini 3
models


       After the definition and parametrization of the bead chemical types, sizes and
labels, the next step is to generate Martini 3 CG models of complex and bigger molecules
using more than one bead. For a quick guide of how to build them, the user can (initially)
follow these 3 steps:
       1) Mapping of the atomistic model to the CG model: split the molecule in
reasonable chemical groups (the building blocks) in accordance with the Martini bead
sizes: R (4-atoms-to-1-bead), S (3-atoms-to-1-bead), and T (2-atoms-to-1-bead). Higher
resolution allows for a better reproduction of symmetry, shape, and chemical details.
Lower resolutions allow for simpler models which are more computationally efficient.
       2) Assignment of chemical bead types: based on the chemical group underlying
the bead, one selects a similar fragment in the “Martini Bible” (Supplementary Tables
24 and 25, with examples of chemical types based on different groups for neutral and
charged fragments, respectively). In case no matching chemical group is present, a
suitable bead type can be selected using the partitioning data of the isolated fragment
(Supplementary Tables 17, 18 and 19).
       3) Get the bonded parameters from atomistic data: bond distances should follow
the center-of-geometry “rule” (Section C3) including the underlying hydrogen atoms.
Appropriate force constants and additional bonded terms such as angles or dihedrals
should be calibrated with respect to structural data obtained from either reference
atomistic simulations or a structural databank.
       For advanced parameterization of Martini 3 CG models, it is important to consider
what we call interaction density: the interaction energies and the bead density (i.e., the
number density of beads) in the model 2. This concept may be considered analogous to
the invariant degree of polymerization concept in polymer field theories, which is a
measure related to the number of intermolecular interactions a given polymer chain
experiences37. A balanced interaction density guarantees that the Martini CG models do
not only have reasonable partitioning and miscibility properties, but also follow correct
trends in self-interaction and solvation free energies. In the following section, we describe

                                                                                          35
in more detail the guidelines summarized above. These guidelines are mainly based on
the main class of molecules used to parametrize the neutral beads: organic molecules
composed of atoms of the second period of the periodic table, and including sulfur,
chloride, and bromide. Some comments about charged molecules and exceptions to the
rules are also discussed. Following the guidelines presented here should provide more
stable, accurate, and compatible models.


C1) Mapping and choice of bead sizes
       Ideally, when generating a Martini CG model, one should avoid dividing chemical
groups (e.g., amides or carboxylates) between two beads. Under- or over-mapping
should also be avoided whenever possible, with the consequences recently described in
Alessandri and Souza et al.2. In addition, it is desirable to retain as much as possible the
volume, shape, and symmetry of the original atomistic molecule. A workflow for mapping
CG models in the Martini 3 framework is presented in Supplementary Figure 6.




           Supplementary Figure 6: General rules for mapping and choice of bead sizes.

                                                                                         36
Default mapping of linear and ring fragments: Like in the previous version, only non-
hydrogen atoms define the mapping. The default option for 4-1, 3-1, and 2-1 mappings
are R-, S- and T-beads, respectively. T-beads are usually better to represent the flatness
of aromatic rings while S-beads better mimic the “bulkier” shape of aliphatic rings. R-
beads are the best option for computational performance, with the bead size reasonably
good to represent 4-1 linear molecules with internal flexibility (e.g., aliphatic chains). As
a generic guideline, the number of beads should be optimized such that the maximum
mismatch in mapping is ±1 non-hydrogen atom per 10 non-hydrogen atoms in the
atomistic structure.

Mapping of branched fragments: Fully branched fragments (such as fragments with
quaternary carbon atoms or tertiary amine groups) should usually use beads of smaller
size, as the central atom is not exposed to the environment, reducing its influence on the
interactions. For instance, a neopentane group contains 5 non-hydrogen atoms but, as it
is fully branched, can be safely modeled as an R-bead. Note that neopentane is, indeed,
more similar to butane than to pentane in terms of boiling point and oil/water transfer free
energies38–40.
       The choice of the bead size for partially branched fragments of molecules is not
straightforward; it is desirable to validate the choice by comparison to experimental data
and/or in relation to other Martini 3 CG models. The main danger of using the “full-
branching rule” for partially branched fragments is to reduce the self-interactions too
much. Such a reduction can be especially problematic in polymeric molecules. Some
other known and useful exceptions for the mapping rules are described below:

Organic compounds with fluorine and iodine: Organofluorines and organoiodines are
two extreme mapping exceptions for halo compounds (modelled by X-type beads). As
fluorine atoms in halo compounds are very small and have weak VdW interactions, we
consider that two fluorine atoms are equivalent to one non-hydrogen atom in these cases.
On the other hand, iodine is big and has very strong dispersive interactions which
increases the self-interaction in most of the compounds containing this heavy atom. So,
in this case, each iodine atom should count as two non-hydrogen atoms in the mapping.
Some choices of chemical bead types for all halogens are presented in section C4.

                                                                                          37
Heavy atoms: Atoms from the 4th or higher periods of the periodic table should be
carefully considered for the mapping and bead size (as they have not been tested), with
the choice validated against experimental data. As default option, we suggest that any
atom in periods higher than 3rd one should count as two non-hydrogen atoms, like in the
case of iodine. Fragments or molecules containing atoms from the 3rd period that are not
well-validated (e.g., silicon) may follow the rules defined in Supplementary Figure 6.

Ions and charged groups in general: A strict mapping rule for charged molecules or
fragments can also be challenging given that such systems follow different trends
compared to the neutral molecules. Localized charged organic groups attached to neutral
fragments could be represented by Q-beads. In these cases, the rules summarized in
Supplementary Figure 6 work reasonably well. For small, spherically symmetric soluble
ions, mapping can vary from 1-1 (e.g. metals) to 7-1 (e.g., hexafluorophosphate). Thus,
having only three different bead size options is, at best, sub-optimal. For these cases, a
precise definition for the best bead sizes and types is described in section C3.
      The rules described here, in particular the workflow illustrated in Supplementary
Figure 6, should be used as standard procedure. They could also be used as starting
point for automated mapping codes such as Auto-Martini41 (for now only working with
Martini 2) or Cartographer (under development in Marrink’s group)42. Further refinement
can always be performed based on the interaction density concept and proper validation
against experimental data.

computationally predict thermodynamic properties. Prediction schemes for partition
coefficients (logP values) usually use connectivity and proximity parameters as
complementary variables in their algorithms43. Even all-atom force-fields have different
LJ parameters and/or partial charges for atoms of the same element and chemical group

C3) Bond distances based on molecular volume and shape
      Angles and dihedrals impact the quality of CG models as they control the
conformational flexibility of the molecule. In Martini 3, bond distances are equally
important, since they will define the volume, shape, and bead density of CG models,
which have important consequences for the overall behavior of the force field. Here, we
first describe a general rule to get reasonably good models, followed by a description for
volume-shape based refinement.


Center-of-geometry mapping: In general, bond distances obtained from center of mass
(COM)-mapped atomistic structures proved unsatisfactory, as they often led to overly high
packing densities. This is due to the geometrical mismatch enforced by neglecting the
lower resolution of CG models (e.g., triangular versus hexagonal shape of benzene) and


                                                                                                            41
by not taking into account the volume of hydrogen atoms. Instead, using a center of
geometry (COG)-based mapping of atomistic structures, taking hydrogen atoms into
account, allows to better reproduce the molecular volume and mass densities. Therefore,
bond distances obtained from COG-mapped atomistic structures constitute the standard
procedure for obtaining bonded parameters in Martini 3.
      COG-mapping is especially important the closer the mapping is to atomistic
resolution, i.e. when using T-beads. COM- and COG-based bond distances are indeed
almost equivalent in the case of a 4-to-1 mapped alkane chain. In contrast, they differ
considerably in the case of 2-to-1 mapped rings. For instance, benzene has a COM bond
length of 0.21 nm, while a COG provides 0.29 nm. COG-based, like COM-based, bond
distances can be directly extracted from atomistic structures. Thus, bond distances
extracted from COG-mapped atomistic structures should be the standard for automated
parametrization schemes, such as the Auto-Martini41 or PyCGtool47 algorithms.

Bonded parameters, force constants, and exclusions: In Martini 3, non-bonded
interactions between connected beads are always excluded, both in the default bonded
terms as in elastic network of proteins. As demonstrated by Alessandri et al.2, in such
condition, weak bond force constants (lower than 700 kJ/mol) should be avoided, as they
may allow the CG beads within a molecule getting too close, resulting in too high
interactions with the environment and causing artifacts (e.g., particles sitting on top of
each other, and excessive aggregation)2.
       Exclusion can also be applied to 2nd or 3rd neighbors to help the representation of
angle and dihedral distributions in certain cases. For rings, even higher orders of
exclusion are used, which can help to keep their conformation numerically stable. In
particular, exclusion always should be used between non-dummy virtual particles (see
section C4) and the reference beads used for their construction.


C4) Numerical stability of simulations
       A last aspect that needs to be carefully considered for Martini 3 models is their
stability in MD simulations. The target is to get models stable using 20 fs as time step, to
optimally profit from the computational speedup while keeping energy drifts within
acceptable boundaries14. This, however, can be a challenge. For instance, DNA and RNA
models based on the previous version of Martini 17,18 were usually only stable with a time
step of 10 fs. The following sections describe the three main approaches that should be
considered for numerically stable simulations: (i) number of constraints and usage of
virtual particles; (ii) dihedrals and 3-body angles around 0°/180°; and (iii) bead masses.
As the choices of mapping and bonded potential can have an influence on (i) and (ii), we

                                                                                         44
advise users to always consider potential problems in numerical instability at the
beginning of the parametrization.


Use of virtual sites to reduce the number of constraints: For stiff molecular fragments,
such as polycyclic compounds requiring more than four Martini particles to be described,
virtual (interaction) sites (VSs)50 are employed to improve the numerical stability of the
models. Martini aromatic ring structures are held together by constraints. As more
extended stiff molecular structures need to be described, the number of constraints that
need to be used in their Martini representations grows. However, an extended network of
constraints leads to numerical instabilities. The use of VSs allows to save on the number
of constraints thereby increasing the numerical stability of models. Besides, it also
improves the computational performance 51.

        As an example, we describe the proposed Martini 3 model for 3-methylindole,
which is used in the description of the amino acid tryptophan. The molecule consists of
10 non-hydrogen atoms, which translates into a 5 T-beads model given the aromaticity of
the molecule. Straightforwardly interconnecting the 5 beads with constraints leads to a
network of 8 constraints (Supplementary Figure 8A). Such a network of constraints is
hard to satisfy: a single molecule in vacuum is still numerically stable with a time step of
20 fs, but a condensed phase containing many copies of such a model readily leads to
                                                                                                        45
numerical instabilities with time steps as low as 10 fs, thus requiring even smaller time
steps51. This makes large-scale simulations impracticable. The idea to overcome this
limitation is inspired by the “hinge” construction used in the latest Martini models of sterols
and hopanoids proposed by Melo and co-workers52. Indeed, another model which can be
devised for 3-methylindole exploits the “hinge” construction for the 4 external beads
(Supplementary Figure 8B) while the central bead is described as a VS, i.e., a particle
whose position is completely defined by its constructing particles (in this case, the other
four beads). Due to the reduction on the number of constraints used (from 8 to 5), the use
of the hinge model also accelerates the calculation: in the case of the same 3-
methylindole condensed phase, the simulation runs ∼1.5 times faster using the same
time step. Note also that an improper dihedral is applied in order to keep the hinge model
flat. More details and further constructions of ring compounds will be described in a future
publication51.


Numerical instabilities caused by dihedral potentials: As described by Bulacu et al.53,
traditional torsion angle potentials (i.e. proper dihedrals) used in atomistic MD simulations
may not always be suitable for modeling CG systems. This is true, for example, in very
flexible CG molecules, for which conformations leading to numerical instabilities of the
dihedral potentials are often encountered.
       Specifically, given a dihedral potential between particles i-j-k-l, numerical
instabilities are encountered when either of the angles i-j-k and j-k-l are 0° or 180°. Bulacu
et al. proposed two solutions53: 1) the use of a newly developed Restricted Bending (ReB)
potential (function type 10 in GROMACS11,12 2016.x and newer releases) to prevent the
i-j-k and j-k-l from exploring conformations at 0° or 180°; 2) the use of virtual, or dummy,
particles in a so-called dummy-assisted dihedral (DAD) to redefine the dihedral in a way
that, for simple geometrical reasons, the angles i-j-k and j-k-l cannot explore the
numerically unstable regions around 0° or 180°.
       Bulacu et al. showcased the use of these approaches in the PEG model, which by
using ReB potentials is numerically stable with a time step of 20 fs, unlike the original
Martini PEG model of Lee et al.54 for which numerical instabilities are often encountered
with a time step of 10 fs53. The ReB potentials prevent three consecutive PEG beads from

                                                                                            46
being colinear, i.e. to reach an angle i-j-k of 180°. The approach was also suggested to
be used in combination with side-chain dihedral corrections in Martini proteins55. In the
DAD approach, the use of virtual sites allows to define dihedrals which would otherwise
not be possible to define based simply on the structure of the CG model 53. The position
of the virtual sites is chosen in a way that will restrict the conformations of the i-j-k and j-
k-l angles to numerically stable regions of the conformational space, i.e. away from 0°
and 180°. The protein backbone53 and polythiophenes56 are examples of models that can
employ dihedrals which use virtual sites.


Masses of the beads in Martini 3: By default, we use standard masses for the three
Martini 3 bead sizes (described in section B1), like in Martini 213. These masses were
chosen for simplicity and their use guarantees no numerical instabilities with a time step
of 20 fs, as validated by all the tests carried out in this work. For analysis of trends in
kinetic properties, simulations should be carried out with more realistic masses. However,
if the realistic mass of a certain molecule or fragment is much lower compared to when
standard masses were used, numerical instabilities may arise also with time steps of 20
fs. This was not extensively tested and we wish to warn users about this possibility.

D1) Oil/water transfer free energies
Thermodynamic integration57 was used to compute free energies of solvation (ΔGS→0).
The solute was solvated in a pre-equilibrated solvent box of size of at least 5x5x5 nm3.
Four solvents were considered: pure water, pure hexadecane, pure chloroform and water-
saturated octanol (~0.25:0.75 water/octanol molar ratio58,59). A minimum of 11 simulations
with equally spaced points going from 0 to 1 were performed, where solute-solvent
interactions were scaled from full (λ=1) to zero solute-solvent interactions (λ=0). A
stochastic integrator60 was employed for the simulations that were equilibrated for 2 ns,
and each λ point was run for a minimum of 10 ns. A soft-core potential (as implemented
in GROMACS11,12) was used to avoid singularities due to solute-solvent particle overlap
as interactions were switched61. The soft-core parameters used were α=0.5 and p=1 (for
the soft-core power). The free energies and corresponding errors were computed using
the Multistate Bennett Acceptance Ratio (MBAR)62. The transfer free energy associated
with transferring a solute from a solvent S1 to a solvent S2 (ΔGS1→S2) is then computed
as the difference ΔGS1→S2 = ΔGS1→0 - ΔGS2→0. Results in water/hexadecane,
water/octanol and water/chloroform were compared with experimental data 21,63–70.


D2) Pure liquids: densities and enthalpies of vaporization
A liquid phase was approximated as an equilibrated box of dimensions of about 5x5x5
nm3. Densities were extracted with the GROMACS 11,12 tool gmx energy, based on the
volume of the simulation box and the real masses of the molecules being simulated (note
that otherwise usually the CG model for a given molecule uses the default, size-
dependent masses of the beads, described in Supplementary Notes, section B2, rather
than the exact mass of the atoms represented by the beads). For the enthalpy of
vaporization, an empty simulation box (7x7x7 nm3) containing one molecule was also built
to mimic the gas phase. Simulations of the liquid phase were equilibrated in the NPT
ensemble at 298 K and 1 bar, while gas phase simulations were simulated in the NVT
ensemble at 298 K. The enthalpy of vaporization (ΔHvap) was computed as ΔHvap ≈ Ugas -

D5) Dimerization PMFs of nucleobases
PMFs were calculated to estimate base pairing free energies of CG models of cytosine
and guanine. The PMF profiles were obtained from umbrella sampling (US) 77 simulations
as described for the parametrization of nucleobases in Martini 217,18. Namely, two bases
were placed in a box with about 500 water beads. For the CG systems, windows were
spaced 0.05 nm apart from 0.3 to 2.0 nm between the centers of mass of the bases. The
distance between the bases was restrained with a harmonic umbrella potential with a
force constant of 2,000 kJ·mol−1·nm−2. Each window was simulated for 100 ns. The
nucleobases were constrained to the same plane using position restraints in the direction
perpendicular to the reaction coordinate. They were aligned so that the X-axis passed
through beads corresponding to the N1 atom of the purines and the N3 atom of the
pyrimidines, and the base plane was set as the XY plane. Position restraints with a force
constant of 4,000 kJ/(mol·nm2) were used in the Y and Z directions. The free energy
profiles were computed using the weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM)78 as
implemented in the GROMACS11,12 tool gmx wham, with the statistical error estimated
from bootstrapping. For the AA systems, PMFs obtained using CHARMM2779,80 and
AMBER99sb_parmbsc081 were retrieved from Uusitalo et al.17, and hence were obtained
as described for the parametrization of the previous Martini models of nucleic acids 17,18.
Note that we did not attempt to correct the PMFs for the biases introduced by the various
constraints, as we only compare different force fields to each other.

D6) Lipid bilayer simulations
We tested 100 different single-component lipid bilayers with Martini 3 and compared the
results to Martini 2. The program insane.py82 was employed to set up the bilayers
containing 242 lipids. All bilayers were built in an initial box of 8.8×8.8×8.0 nm³ and
solvated with ~3,000 CG water beads. For anionic lipids, the bilayer was neutralized with
Na+ ions before adding the solvent. The temperature in the simulation box was controlled
by a velocity rescale thermostat83 (reference temperature Tref = 310 K, coupling time
constant τT = 1 ps). Semi-isotropic pressure coupling with a compressibility of 3·10 −4 bar−1
was employed. During the equilibration procedure, the pressure was controlled by the
Berendsen barostat84 (reference pressure pref = 1 bar; coupling time constant τp = 1 ps),
while the Parrinello-Rahman barostat85 was used during the production (pref = 1 bar; τp =
12 ps). After a steepest decent minimization of 500 steps, the system was equilibrated
for 500 ps (time step of 10 fs) and 30 ns (time step of 20 fs) using a leap-frog integrator.
Production runs of 1 µs were performed with a time step of 20 fs. For all systems, we
analyzed the average area per lipid, area compressibility, average tail order parameter,
and bilayer thickness. All reported errors are standard errors of the mean obtained from
block averaging.
A slightly different procedure was used to study the temperature dependence of lipid
bilayers properties. The initial conditions were generated using Martinate (see
Supplementary Codes, section H1) for the desired temperature. The generated initial
bilayer patches had sizes 10⨉10 nm with the bilayer repeat distance of 10 nm. The
systems were equilibrated at their respective temperatures for 1 ns prior to production
simulations of 1 µs. The area per lipid from simulations was calculated from the lipid
bilayer area by dividing it by the number of lipids per leaflet. The lipid bilayer area was
calculated using FatSlim86 using phosphate “PO4” beads to construct the surface.

D9) Osmotic coefficients of ionic solutions
The osmotic coefficient was computed from simulations adopting the protocol originally
proposed by Luo and Roux31. A rectangular box was created in which the solute
molecules were confined in z-direction by a flat-bottomed potential to the center of the
box. At a distance of 2.52078 nm from the center of the box, a harmonic force with a force
constant 1000 kJ/nm2 was applied to the solute molecules. The box dimensions were
taken to be 10.08312 nm in z dimensions and 5.04156 nm in x and y. Previous to each
run, a random configuration of solute and solvent molecules was created with polyply
(https://github.com/marrink-lab/polyply_1.0) placing solute molecules only in the center of
the box and the solvent in the entire box. After energy minimization, this setup was
subjected to a 10 ns equilibration using Berendsen barostat84. Production simulations
were run for 500 ns as previously used for atomistic simulation29 at a pressure of 1 bar
and coupling to a temperature of 298.15 K using the velocity rescale algorithm by Bussi
et al.83   Following Sauter and Grafmuller 97,98, the pressure was coupled only in z-
dimensions with Parrinello-Rahman pressure coupling85. In the original implementation31
the simulation was run in an NVT ensemble. PME93 was used in all simulations.
The osmotic pressure was computed from the trajectory by recalculating the total force
exerted by the solute particles onto the flat-bottomed potential averaged over the two
potentials. Subsequently that force is divided by the xy area of the box. The ensemble
average as well as an error were computed from the time-series of the osmotic pressure

                                                                                        54
values. We verified that our setup yields accurate results by reproducing the osmotic
pressure of NaCl at 1 M using CHARMM27 from the original paper 31. Having obtained
the osmotic pressure the osmotic coefficient was computed by dividing the osmotic
pressure by the ideal pressure at that given total concentration31. We also ran a second
replica of each simulation given the importance of replicas in MD simulations99. However,
as previously observed97, the convergence is fast and all values were reproducible within
the statistical error of the first run. All statistical errors were less than 1.4%. In total the
osmotic coefficient was computed for 10 systems: 5 ion pairs (sodium chloride, potassium
chloride, sodium glutamate, lysine chloride, arginine chloride) and 5 zwitterionic
molecules (alanine, glycine, valine, serine, threonine) with no net charge. With the
exception of valine, all coefficients were calculated at a total molar concentration of 1 M.
For the purpose of comparing to previous atomistic simulation data the concentration of
valine was 0.6 M. It should be noted that the zwitterionic part of the amino acids is
represented by a neutral bead (P6), which means these molecules are neutral in the
simulation. More information about osmotic coefficients is presented in Supplementary
Results, section F3.

D11) Aedamers: PMF and self-assembly of DAN and NDI tetramers
PMFs were calculated using US77, extracting the free energies by WHAM78, as
implemented by the Grossfield lab101. Pegylated monomers of dialkoxynaphthalene
(DAN) and naphthalene diimide (NDI) were placed in a 7.8x7.8x7.8 nm3 simulation box
with around 3920 water beads using the GROMACS tools11,12 gmx solvate and gmx
insert-molecules. The reaction coordinate was the distance between the centers of
aromatic cores; 20-25 windows spaced 0.1 nm apart were used, with a simple harmonic
umbrella potential with a force constant of 100 kJ/(mol·nm2). The minimum sampling time
for each window was 60 ns. The PMFs were used to calculate free energies of
dimerization presented in Figure 2A of the main text. Statistical errors were estimated
from bootstrapping. The DAN and NDI cores of the pegylated molecules were used to
build DAN and NDI tetramers that are connected by charged amide linkers. Experimental
work demonstrated that these molecules associate more strongly upon elongation: when
longer chains dimerize, they form more DAN-NDI contacts102–104. One DAN and one NDI
tetramer were placed in a 10x10x10 nm3 simulation box with 8090 water beads,
neutralized with 10 Na+ ions. After minimization and equilibration, a total of 100,000
structures sampled at regular intervals from 20 µs of simulation, taken after the dimer was
formed, were clustered. A snapshot of one of the most populated canonically fully
alternating structures is presented in the main manuscript. Further details about the CG
models, atomistic simulations used as reference and extra details of the analysis
performed are described in Supplementary Results, section F4.

D13) TM proteins and dimerization PMFs
We computed dimerization free energies for four selected transmembrane (TM) helices:
the TM domains of the receptor tyrosine kinases EphA1 (PDB id: 2K1L)108 and ErbB1
(PDB id: 2M0B)109; the red blood cell protein glycophorin A (GpA, PDB id: 1AFO)110; as
well as the well-known model peptide WALP23111,112. For each of the four peptide dimers,
we estimated the dimerization free energies by calculating the PMFs with the US
technique and WHAM78.
For EphA1 and ErbB1, we followed the setup used in Javanainen et al.16. The simulation
models followed experiments113,114 as closely as possible: both dimers were embedded
in a DLPC bilayer comprised of 400 lipids, solvated by 5,000 water beads, and either
~150 mM (EphA1) or ~500 mM (ErbB1) of NaCl. The systems were generated using
insane.py82. Equilibration simulations of ~50 ns were first performed on a restrained dimer
and then on an unrestrained dimer. The 2D inter-peptide center-of-mass (COM) distance
was used as a reaction coordinate (ζ). 15 frames were chosen as the umbrella sampling
windows, with reference values for ζ ranging from 0.6 to 3.4 nm with a spacing of 0.2 nm.
The spring constant of the umbrella potential was set to 400 kJ/(mol·nm2). The initial
frames were taken from the work of Javanainen et al.16, also available in the Zenodo
repository115, and were built based on the dimer conformations determined by NMR108,109.



                                                                                        57
GpA simulations were based on the approach of Domanski et al., with the reaction
coordinate defined as the difference in distance between helix−helix native contacts of
the GXXXG motif (DRMS)116,117. GpA was inserted into a POPC bilayer using
g_membed118. The simulation box contained 25 POPC lipids and over 1,400 coarse-
grained water beads. The US simulations were set up with 40 replicas spaced between
DRMS 0 and 2.5 nm with sampling umbrella position and spring constants as defined by
Domanski et al.117. The WHAM method was used to perform the unbiasing of the US
trajectories78, using the Grossfield lab implementation 101. The dimerization PMF obtained
was projected onto the radial center-of-mass distance coordinate ζ.
For WALP23, the simulation protocol followed previous calculations carried out with
version 2.0 of the Martini force field by Castillo et al.119. Single-component POPC and 1,2-
dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC) membranes comprised of 350 lipids were
generated using insane.py82 and solvated by 4400 water particles and 150 mM NaCl.
Peptides were embedded in the membranes in a parallel or anti-parallel setting, at
distances between 0.6 and 3.5 nm, using the GROMACS tool gmx insert-molecules,
yielding a total number of 30 starting structures for each system. Lipids overlapping with
the peptides were automatically deleted by GROMACS11,12 gmx insert-molecules.
Additionally, a few lipids were deleted to ensure bilayer symmetry and a fixed number of
lipids in all systems. Each starting structure was then equilibrated for 200 ns using the 2D
inter-peptide COM distance as a harmonic restraint, with a force constant of 1500
kJ/(mol·nm2). The same distance was used as a reaction coordinate ζ in the US
production runs. The PMFs were calculated using gmx wham120 bundled with
GROMACS11,12.
The free energies of association were calculated from the PMFs, after adding the
Jacobian (also called entropic) correction RTln(r)121,122. Further details about the
dimerization free energy calculations are described in Supplementary Results, section
F5. The temperatures of the peptides, the membrane, and the solvent were maintained
separately at 303 K (for EphA1 and ErbB1) or 310 K (for GpA and WALP23). Pressure
was controlled semi-isotropically with a reference pressure of 1 bar, compressibility of
3x10-4 bar-1, and a time constant of 12 ps. Production runs were carried out for 10 µs
(EphA1 and ErbB1), 12 µs (WALP23) or 8 µs (via replica exchange for GpA) for each

umbrella window, using a time step of 20 fs; the initial 10-50 ns were discarded from
analyses.


D14) Protein hydration and water flux in FraC
The wild-type Fragaceatoxin C (FraC) structure was taken from the PDB database
(4TSY)123. Missing residues at the C-terminus were modeled using VMD49. The
secondary structure was determined using DSSP 124,125 and the added residues were
manually set to helical. Elastic Networks92 were applied to each monomer of the protein
complex, with a distance cutoff of 0.8 nm using a force constant of 1000 kJ/(mol·nm2).
Additional harmonic bonds were added between residues M48-W149 and T50-N147 of
neighboring monomers, mimicking hydrogen bonds between their backbones’ amide
groups. The bilayer, built using the program insane.py82, was composed of 50 mol%
DOPC and 50 mol% DPSM (N-stearoyl-D-erythro-sphingosylphosphorylcholine). The
protein was inserted into the center of the bilayer, with its geometrical principal component
aligned with the membrane normal. The system was solvated using water and 150 mM
NaCl. The final dimensions of the system were 17 nm in z and 14.5 nm in x and y,
containing a total of 335 DOPC, 335 DPSM lipids, 17,248 waters, 162 Na+ cations and
218 Cl- anions. All simulations with this system were performed at 310 K and a pressure
of 1 bar. Electrostatics were solved using PME93 due to the high amount of charge in the
FraC pore. The system was energy minimized using steepest descent for 10,000 steps.
Equilibration was performed in three steps. First the system was simulated using a time
step of 2 fs and the Berendsen barostat 84 (semi-isotropic in xy, z) with a coupling time
constant of 8 ps, while keeping the protein constrained to its initial position. Second, the
protein was unconstrained. Third, a larger time step of 10 fs was used to equilibrate the
pressure. After 30,000 steps the final production was started for 1 μs with a time step of
20 fs, using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat85 with a coupling time constant of 12 ps.
Hydration was visually inspected using VMD49. The flux of particles through the protein
pore was calculated using fluxer.py (https://github.com/mnmelo/fluxer). For the fluxer
program, the pore was treated as toroidal with a bilayer thickness of 4 nm. The reported
flux is the sum of the absolute flux in both z directions, otherwise it would always be zero



                                                                                          59
at equilibrium. The flux was calculated for water, Na+ and Cl- over the complete production
run.


D15) A2A receptor and ion binding
The CG model of the apo adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) was based on the crystal
structure (PDB code 3RFM)126. Mutations were reverted to the wild type sequence and
the missing loop (extracellular loop 2 - ECL2), was modeled using the pdb structure 2YDO
as reference127. As G protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) are conformationally flexible
depending on their environment and association of ligands and other proteins 128–132, we
used a shorter distance cutoff of 0.7 nm for the Elastic Network92, and a force constant of
700 kJ/(mol·nm2) (rather than the standard 500 kJ/(mol·nm2)). Even in this situation, the
average RMSD of the backbone in relation to the crystal structure was 3.5 Å, which is in
good agreement with atomistic simulations 133. To mimic the effect of the hydration in the
core and in the buried ligand pocket of A2AR, a tiny CG water bead (representing one to
two water molecules) was placed inside the protein, bound to Asn181 and His250.
Additional bonds were also added between the residues Thr88-Trp246 and Asn181-
His250, representing some water bridges. These four extra interactions were modelled
with a harmonic potential using bond distances based on the reference crystal structure
and a weak force constant of 500 kJ/(mol·nm2) without excluding the non-bonded
interactions. The protonation states of the residues were checked using the H++
server134. In accordance with the obtained predictions, Glu13 and His278 were
considered charged. Asp52 was also kept charged as it is the main residue involved in
the interaction with one Na+ ion in the core of this GPCR135. The protein was initially
placed in a POPC lipid bilayer using the program insane.py82. CG water particles were
also included, and the ionic concentration was set to 150 mM NaCl. As in all other
simulations of this work, sodium and chloride ions are represented by TQ5 beads with
charge +1 and -1, respectively. The final dimensions of the simulation box were about
12x12x12 nm3. After minimization (500 steps of steepest descent) and 100 ns
equilibration (time step of 10 fs), a production run was performed for 5 µs. Both
equilibration and production simulations were performed at 310 K and 1 bar using PME 93
to calculate the electrostatic interactions.

F2) Hofmeister series, ionic-liquids, and validation of charged beads.
       The Hofmeister series is originally defined as a classification of ions in order of
their ability to affect the solubility of proteins3. Nowadays, the concept can be considered
extended to other systems 156, including ionic liquids94. Here we calibrated and validated
the Martini ions in terms of ion transfer between a water phase and an ionic liquid (IL)
phase. After bead assignment based on anion exchange simulations, some pure ionic
liquids were also tested.


CG IL models: Two IL classes were modelled, based on organophosphonium (in
particular, trihexyltetradecylphosphonium [P66614]+) and 1-3-dialkylimidazolium ([Cnmim]+)
cations. The mapping and bead chemical types and sizes are shown in Supplementary
Figure 16. The [P66614]+ model shows some similarities, in terms of mapping, with the
models of Schaeffer et al.157 A central +1 charged Q1 bead represents the tetramethyl
phosphonium fragment. The imidazolium charged ring was built on top of the prototype
model developed with the open-beta version of Martini 3 (see Vazquez-Salazar et al.158).
In the current version, the 1,3 dimethyl imidazolium ring was modelled with three T-beads,
using the bead type TN1q for the nitrogen-containing aromatic fragments while the rest
of the carbon-based aromatic ring is represented by a TC6 bead. Each TN1q bead has a
+0.5 partial charge (see Section A4). This template was used for all ([Cnmim]+ cations,
except ([C2mim]+, for which the extra carbon was mimicked by changing one of the
nitrogen-containing fragments to SN1q. The alkyl fragments connected to the charged
cores of [P66614]+ and [Cnmim]+ were modelled with C3 type, with size adapted to map the
exact length of the alkyl fragment. The remaining saturated alkyl tails of the cations are
represented by C1/C2 beads, as typically used for alkanes in Martini 3. Bonded
parameters involving the charged fragments are based on atomistic trajectories
generated with OPLS parameters adapted by Padua et al.159 to ionic liquids. They were
tuned to get the best compromise in terms of bulk density and molecular surface area
(see Section C3). Bonded parameters of the alkyl chain are based on the models of
alkanes of Martini 3. Sodium ion is modelled with charge +1 and TQ5 bead. The small
anions were modelled with single beads, with the best bead assignment described in
Supplementary Figure 17C.

Anion transfer in IL/water biphasic systems: In the presence of inorganic salts, some
water-miscible ILs can form stable biphasic systems with water. Importantly, the
properties of the IL/aqueous biphasic mixture formed are greatly affected by the salt
used94. One of these properties is the anion exchange between the IL and the water
phase, which will depend on differences in hydrophilicities of the anions. Analogous to
classical oil/water partitioning systems, used in the parametrization and bead assignment
of neutral beads, anion exchange between IL and water phases was one of the systems
used to model charged beads in Martini 3. Supplementary Figures 17A-17B
summarizes the main results. Initially, two IL/water biphasic systems were tested,
[P66614][Q3]/ NaB(aq) and [C4mim][Q1]/ NaB(aq). At this point of the Martini 3
development, Q3 and Q1 beads were used as generic anions for the IL phase. The “B” is
the anion of the sodium salt, in the water phase, which was defined with all possible
charged beads in Martini 3, including divalent D-beads. Details of the simulation are
described in section D8. The plots in the right panels of Supplementary Figure 17A-
17B show the bead density of “B” anions in each phase for both IL/water systems. Overall,
hydrophilic (or hard) charged beads as D and Q5 type hardly exchange with the anions
in the IL phase. On the other hand, soft anions prefer the IL phase. Different ILs showed
some differences in the behavior. Hydrophilic ions tends to prefer the interface when the
                                                                                                      72
IL is based on [P66614]+. As [C4mim]+ is more hydrophilic than [P66614]+, it is notable a
slightly higher preference of the hydrophilic ions to migrate to the IL based on [C4mim]+.
Considering the trends observed and comparison with experimental data 94,96, it is
possible to define a Hofmeister series for the charged-beads, which was used to define
the chemical types for a lot of anions, as shown in Supplementary Figure 17C. Based
on these bead assignments, specific IL/water mixtures were simulated and compared to
experimental work of Dupont et al.94 and Naert et al.96 (see Supplementary Figure 2C
of the main manuscript).

Densities of pure ionic liquids: Pure ionic liquids were also used for validation of the
charged beads, based on the same simulation protocol described in Vazquez-Salazar et
al.158. Different temperatures at a pressure of 1 bar were tested, spanning from 293 to
363 K (see the additional Supplementary Sheet File for more details). Overall, the
results of our MD simulations (see Supplementary Figure 17D) indicate that the Martini
3 models show accurate densities that correlate well with the experimental data 160–164.
For imidazolium based ILs, we tested cations with different tail lengths mixed with [BF4]-
anion. Densities were only slightly overestimated, with R2 of 0.98 and MAPE of 2.0%. For
organophosphonium based ionic liquids, the same cation - [P66614]+ – was mixed with
different anions: [PF6]-, [Br]-, [BF4]- and [Cl]-. The results show a R2 of 0.91 and MAPE of
6.7%. The slightly lower accuracy in the organophosphonium IL case reflects the limits of
representing different anions only using single beads in three defined sizes. For instance,
in [P66614][PF6] case, a regular Q1 bead is too small to represent the [PF6]- anion, resulting
in too high densities (around +10%). On the other hand, a T-bead is too big to represent
the [Cl]- anion in [P66614][Cl], so the densities in this case are underestimated (around -
8%).

F3) Osmotic coefficients of ionic solutions


       The osmotic pressure is directly related to the chemical potential of the solvent in
a mixture. Thus it is a very robust quantity for estimating solubility of molecules for low to
moderately high concentrations. For example, it has previously been used as a target
quantity to optimize Martini 2 sugars, which were seen to aggregate too much 98. It has
also been used in atomistic force-field optimization29,31,97, because it allows to adjust
parameters for ionic species for which no reliable solvation free energies or partition
coefficients are available. Experimentally, the osmotic pressure is often not obtained
directly but rather the osmotic coefficient is measured. The osmotic coefficient is the ratio
of the measured osmotic pressure and the osmotic pressure of an ideal solution at a given
concentration. A too large osmotic coefficient is often indicative of an overestimation of
solvation, whereas a too low osmotic coefficient indicates unphysical aggregation of the
solute31,98.
       Supplementary Figure 18 shows the osmotic coefficient of 10 molecules
simulated using Martini 3 and a recently published version of Amber called DES-Amber165
against experimental data166–172. The data is split into zwitterionic molecules and
molecules that possess a net charge, including two inorganic salts, NaCl and KCl. For
each system, the total concentration of solute species in the water solution was 1.0 M,
except for valine, which was simulated at 0.6 M to match the concentration of the
experimental reference. Zwitterionic amino acids, with neutral side-chain, possess no net
charge and in Martini 3 are represented as completely neutral beads. They are indicated
by the red squares in the plot below. The osmotic coefficient of these molecules is overall
very close to the experimental coefficients with the largest deviation being observed for
serine. In this case, Martini 3 osmotic coefficient was 1.025 whereas experimentally an
osmotic coefficient of 0.915 is observed. In general, all osmotic coefficients in Martini 3
are larger than those experimentally observed. This indicates a lack of aggregation and
a tendency to overestimate solubility at these higher concentrations. However, in
comparison to the atomistic DES-Amber force-field, which has been specifically optimized
against this data, for the neutral molecules Martini 3 shows similar deviations. The largest
deviation in DES-Amber is observed for valine (1.011 for atomistic model vs. 1.1 in the

                                                                                           75
experiments). Thus deviations in Martini for this set of molecules are comparable to state
of the art atomistic force-fields, and overall not considered problematic.




Supplementary Figure 18: Comparison between the simulated and the experimental osmotic coefficients
for neutral zwitterionic amino acids and some inorganic and organic (charged amino acids) salt solutions.
Martini 3 (red) and atomistic DES-Amber (blue) are compared in the plot.


        The osmotic pressure coefficients for the charged species, which are composed
of ion pairs, are worse for both the atomistic force-field and Martini 3. Here the largest
deviation for both force-fields is observed for arginine. Interestingly the osmotic coefficient
of sodium chloride is actually closer to experiment than for DES-Amber. However, other
species such as lysine, glutamate, and potassium chloride perform significantly worse
with osmotic coefficients larger by a factor of about 1.2-2.0. However, deviations in the
osmotic pressure by this factor range are also not uncommon for atomistic force-fields,
which are not optimized against this data. For example, the osmotic pressure of glucose
within the GLYCAM06 force-field is also a factor of 2 larger than experiment 97.
        In addition, there are some problems that come inherently with the CG
representation of lysine, arginine and glutamate. It is known that arginine can form

                                                                                                      76
specific stacking interactions173,174. These interactions are probably less well represented
in Martini as the whole guanidinium group is represented by a spherical SQ3p bead. On
the other hand, for lysine it is expected that the charged side-chain can coordinate with
the carboxylic acid of the zwitterionic backbone part of the molecule170. In the current
parametrization, this is counteracted by an angle potential and the fact that the
zwitterionic part is not charged. Motivated by this reasoning, we have computed the
osmotic coefficient of lysine at 0.5M total concentration, which yields an osmotic pressure
coefficient of 1.16 compared to the experimental value of about 0.92. This is already a
large improvement indicating that the Martini single amino acids perform better at lower
concentrations. In general, a better representation of the zwitterionic part of all single
amino-acids, by for example using a polarizable bead is expected to largely improve the
osmotic pressure values. Similar strategies can also be applied for ions and charged
amino acids, for which polarizable versions also existed in Martini 236,175.


F4) Aedamers: atomistic simulations, CG models, and additional analysis.


Reference atomistic simulations: Atomistic simulations of aedamers were run with a
time-step of 2.5 fs, integrating the equations of motion with the leap-frog algorithm176 as
implemented in GROMACS 2016.x and 2018.x11,12. Temperature and pressure (isotropic
coupling to 1 bar) were maintained using the Berendsen coupling schemes 84, with
coupling time constants of 0.3 and 0.5 ps for temperature and pressure, respectively; the
compressibility was set to 4.6 10-5 bar-1. Translation of the center of mass was removed
every 10 steps. The interaction parameters for the molecules were based on the
GROMOS force fields177,178, with modified charges on the aromatic moieties (see below).
For the pegylated aedamers (DANP and NDIP), the 2016H66 version178 was used
because of its superior hydration free energy of ethylene glycol chains. For the amide-
linked aedamers (nDAN and nNDI), the G53a6 set177 was used. For water, the SPC
model179 was used, and counter ions (Na+) were added to the amide-linked aedamers to
neutralize the system; in addition, in some simulations, additional Na + and Cl- ions were
added. All bond lengths were constrained using the LINCS algorithm180, with settings
lincs-order=4 and lincs-iter=1. The SETTLE algorithm181 was used to treat SPC water as

                                                                                         77
rigid. The Verlet update scheme182 was used with a buffer tolerance of 0.005 kJ.mol -1.ps-
1
, updating the neighbor list every 10 steps. The cut-off is 1.4 nm for both Lennard-Jones
(LJ) and coulomb interactions. The coulomb interactions were treated with the reaction
field modification due to Tironi et al.183 This method alleviates cut-off artifacts by ensuring
that both potential and force decay smoothly to zero at the cut-off of 1.4 nm. The
parameter for the bulk dielectric constant (epsilon_rf in GROMACS) was set to 62. Partial
charges on the DAN and NDI cores were calculated using the quantum chemistry
GAMESS-UK package184 by performing the Dipole Preserving Charge (DPC) analysis 7
on the charge density calculated in vacuo using Density Functional Theory (DFT). The
level of DFT employed here used the PBE correlation functional and the PBE0 exchange
functional, with 25% Hartree-Fock exchange185, employing the 6-31g* basis set.


CG aedamer models: The Martini 3 DAN core representation was based on the scheme
for naphthalene51, which is analogous to the scheme described in detail in Section C4
for 3-methyl-indole, and uses four beads that form a rectangle and three beads
constructed from the positions of these four beads. Supplementary Figure 19 shows the
mapping scheme and bead assignment of the DANP molecule. The four corners are
formed by the beads marked TC5 and TC5e on the corners of the naphthalene unit. The
distances between the adjacent corners are constrained and are taken from the Martini 3
naphthalene model 51. As noted, the model uses the “hinge” construction described in
Section C4 for 3-methylindole. Furthermore, the ether bridged substituents at the 2 and
6 positions of the naphthalene ring (marked TN2a) are also placed by a virtual site
construction from the four rectangular core beads, ensuring that there exists a close
relationship between the positions of the atoms in an atomistic model and the beads in
the Martini 3 model. Note that the masses of the virtual beads are distributed among the
actual beads to maintain the total mass of the molecule. By the construction described,
the seven beads that make up the DAN core are always close to being in one plane.

Supplementary Results G) Revisiting the Pitfalls of Martini

       In this section, we present additional quantitative measurements of properties that
are known limitations of the Martini force field. Overall, some aspects have clearly
improved, as the reported deviations from trends in partitioning free energy2 (revisited in
section G1). Other properties such as the temperature dependence did not change in
relation to Martini 2, with the model capturing reasonably well trends in certain
thermodynamic and structural properties, but lacking the temperature sensitivity of
atomistic models (sections G2 and G3). The entropic driving force underlying the
hydrophobic effect is still a limitation in Martini 3 and CG models in general (section G4).
Below, we provide more details for all of these aspects. The reader should keep these
limitations of transferability of Martini 3 in mind when using the new version of the model.

G1) Short bond lengths: effects on oil/water partitioning
       The usage of bead sizes in the previous version of Martini was only connected to
the topology of the molecules: linear or branched chains were modeled with regular beads
while rings used smaller beads. Given that the parametrization at that point was mainly
carried out using linear molecules, 4-1 mapping, regular beads, and a number of standard
bonded parameters, artifacts were noticed for molecules deviating from these conditions,
but using the same building blocks2. In particular, Martini 2 models that use shorter bond
lengths than the standard values (around 0.47 nm) show deviations in the expected
trends of oil/water transfer free energies (ΔGoil→W). The CG models with short bond lengths
(lower than 0.40 nm) usually involve molecules that are overmapped (i.e., use regular
beads for 3-1 or 2-1 mapping) and/or branched. Experimental evidence indicates that
these smaller molecules are always more hydrophilic than linear molecules with the same
chemical group (i.e. same/similar bead chemical type). For water/hexadecane ΔGoil→W,
Martini 2 qualitatively reproduces this trend, but shows substantial deviations in the
extremes of the hydrophobicity scale: smaller hydrophobic molecules tend to be too
hydrophobic, while small hydrophilic ones too hydrophilic. Quantitative empirical
observation indicates the opposite behavior: hydrophobic molecules get from 3.0 to 3.5
kJ/mol more hydrophilic for each aliphatic carbon atom removed, while hydrophilic
molecules get a somewhat smaller free energy gain (2−2.5 kJ/mol.) 2. The discrepancies

                                                                                         84
related to short bond lengths are drastic when one of the solvent phases consists of a CG
model with short bond lengths, such as benzene. In this case, water/benzene ΔGoil→W
show the opposite trend in all the studied cases, with smaller molecules more
hydrophobic than the bigger ones with the same chemical group. Although benzene is
modeled by S-beads in Martini 2137, the interaction between R and S beads was the same
as the ones used in R-R interactions (see some comments about this feature in section
B1). More details about these findings and the reasons behind these problems connected
to the bond lengths are described in the work of Alessandri et al.2.
        In Martini 3, these discrepancies are largely solved, as shown in Supplementary
Figure 21. Models with bond lengths shorter than 0.47 nm are more hydrophilic, with the
stronger effect in hydrophobic molecules, as observed in experimental data 2. The key
reasons for these improvements in Martini 3 are the new interaction balance of organic
beads in different sizes (see section B2) and new mapping rules (see section C1), which
changed the usage of bead sizes: they not only consider the topology, but also consider
the number of non-hydrogen atoms mapped by the CG bead. So, in Supplementary
Figure 21, a two-bead model with bond length 0.47 nm is represented by two R-beads
while 0.40, 0.35, and 0.25 nm use R-S, S-S and T-T bead sizes, respectively.

G2) Temperature dependence of pure solvent systems
      In this section, a number of results pertaining to solvent properties in the Martini 3
force field were collected, compared to atomistic models and experimental data and
discussed. The main focus will be on the temperature dependence, i.e. the temperature
transferability of the models. Properties were calculated for pure water and pure n-octane
in the default 4-1 resolutions: W bead for water and C1-C1 for n-octane. The atomistic
models SPC179 and TIP3P190 for water and the GROMOS 54a7177 and CHARMM36m44
force fields were also considered for comparison. These were simulated with settings
commonly used in GROMOS (reaction field electrostatics, coulomb and LJ cutoffs of 1.4
nm) and CHARMM (PME, and LJ cutoff of 1.2 nm, force switch modifier starting from 1.0
nm) model simulations, respectively. The main experimental references were the NIST
Chemistry WebBook191 and the work of Banipal et al.192.
      Supplementary Figures 22A and 22B show molecular volume in the different
models as a function of temperature for water and n-octane, respectively. In the case of
the Martini 3 water model, the volume per water bead is divided by 4, for comparison. The
volumes of all water models are similar in the region around room temperature (300 K)
and match the experiment well. Atomistic and Martini 3 water models show a small
discrepancy in the volume heat expansion coefficients (αp, related to the slope of the
curve) in relation to the experimental results. For n-octane, it is observed that the
molecular volume in Martini 3 underestimates the volume compared to experimental data
(Supplementary Figure 22B). It is worth noticing that the volumes of the two atomistic
models differ considerably at room temperature, with the GROMOS model being closer

number of vibrations that contribute R per vibration to the Cp in a classical mechanical
treatment by the equipartition theorem. Accounting for the fact that only C-H bond
stretches are constraint, the vibrational contribution is approximately 450 J/(mol∙K).

studied n-octane in different atomistic force fields as a function of temperature 193 and
concluded that in general, united-atom models represent it better than all-atom models.

atomistic water. The process for the Martini 3 beads represents evaporation of clusters
of 4 molecules. These bundles retain cohesive energy and are therefore expected to have
a less negative free energy of solvation. Such comparison was done for the bundled water
molecule195, and this comparison for 4-1 mapped water revealed that the solvation free

similar. Thus, the entropy of solvation, which is the negative slope of the free energy of
solvation with temperature is similar for all models. This implies that the differences in the
free energy values themselves is predominantly a reflection of the differences in enthalpy
per molecule of the models.

G3) Temperature dependence of lipid bilayers
        The area per phospholipid is well known to vary with temperature from
experiments152,198. We have verified that Martini 3 follows the temperature dependence
of this bilayer property by comparing our simulations to the known values of three chosen
phospholipid bilayers in Supplementary Figure 23. The slopes of the relation between
the calculated areas and the temperature are in excellent agreement with experiments.
While most values also fall well within the error estimates of the experimentally
determined areas, there is still room for improvement involving further optimization of the
bonded parameters of the phospholipids. Methods used are described in section D6.

        In this section the hydrophobic interaction is assessed, i.e. the interaction between
two hydrophobic molecules (in this case n-octane) in aqueous solution. It is assessed by
comparing potentials of mean force (PMFs) between two n-octane molecules in water
solvent at different temperatures. The results comparing atomistic and Martini CG models
are presented in Supplementary Figure 24.

       It can be seen from Supplementary Figure 24A that the interaction between two
n-octane molecules in water at the united-atom model level of GROMOS becomes
stronger at higher temperature. This is considered a hallmark of the hydrophobic
interaction199–202. The effect is strongest at the lower temperatures, and levels off above
300 K. The PMFs for two n-octane molecules in water in the all-atom CHARMM36m force
field (Supplementary Figure 24B) are comparable to those in the GROMOS 54a7 force
field. The binding is stronger at higher temperature. The minima are somewhat deeper at
higher temperatures and the temperature effect becomes relatively small above 280 K
instead of 300 K. It can be seen in Supplementary Figure 24C that in the standard
Martini 3 model, the interaction between two n-octane molecules in water is only weakly
temperature dependent. The interaction appears to be somewhat weaker at higher
temperatures. This is in contradiction to the atomistic reference, showing clearly that
Martini 3 cannot properly model the hydrophobic interaction. Direct comparisons in the
physiological temperature range at 300 and 320 K shown in Supplementary Figures
24A-24E, respectively, shows that the strength of the interaction is 1-2 kJ/mol too strong
in the Martini 3 models compared to GROMOS and CHARMM36m, but the balance of
interactions leads to very similar behavior in the different water resolutions of Martini 3.
The correspondence is also better at 320 K than at 300 K due to the relatively weak
temperature dependence in the Martini 3 model versus the atomistic models in which the
interaction is stronger at higher temperature.
       It can be seen in Supplementary Figure 24F that in the polarizable Martini 2
model5 the interaction between two n-octane molecules in water is also only weakly
temperature dependent. Interestingly, the interaction is strongest at 300 K, and a little
weaker both at higher and at lower temperatures. Further improvements in polarizable
Martini water models5,6 maybe allow future Martini models capture the overall trend
observed in atomistic models. Another option would be to develop a Martini model with
temperature dependent potentials, as already demonstrated in bottom-up CG models203.
       Lastly, it is worth mentioning that the tests performed here represent the behavior
of n-octane in water in the limit of very low dilution. As such, they are only valid for this
specific state. At higher concentrations, experiments demonstrate that increasing the


                                                                                          92
temperature leads to higher miscibility in water/alkane biphasic mixtures, reducing the
interfacial tension141 and increasing the solubility of alkanes 204 longer than 5 carbon
atoms in water. For long chains, the cavity costs win in relation to the hydrophobic effect,
explaining this trend205. This is the behavior observed in Martini models, as demonstrated
by Ndao et al.76 where higher temperatures reduce the interfacial tension and increases
the mixing of water/oil biphasic systems. It also explains why trends in membrane bilayers
with higher temperatures (section G3) are well captured.

Supplementary Codes H) Tools and examples of usage


       The Martini 3 force field comes with updates of several of the tools that were
developed to assist users in deploying the models, notably martinize for coarse graining
atomistic structures, and martinate for running and managing simulations.


H1) Martinize2
       The Martini 3 force field is fully integrated in martinize2, which replaces the earlier
martinize program36 to create simulation input files, both structures and topologies, from
atomistic structures. The program and the underlying library vermouth are currently being
finalized and will be described in detail elsewhere (to be published in Kroon et al. 2020).
In short, martinize2 separates the parameters within residues (fragments) from those
bridging residues (links) and choses the correct combinations using graph-based
algorithms. This allows it to generate simulation input files for arbitrary polymers (linear,
branched, cyclic, etc.) using a single set of simple data files. The program is capable of
generating atomistic, united atom and coarse-grain models using a combination of
subgraph matching and resolution mapping. It is worth noting that all data files used by
the program are human readable and writable.
       By separating the data from the algorithms, martinize2 allows for rapid exploration
of simulation parameters, and facilitates the setup of simulations of complex system,
which are up until now out of reach. In this work, all protein simulations were set up using
martinize2 and the program was used for effective exploration of protein parameters. The
martinize2 tool and the supporting vermouth library can be obtained and installed from
the the github repository https://www.github.com/marrink-lab/vermouth-martinize or
through the PyPi repository using


pip install vermouth


H2) Martinate
       Martinate is a flexible, automated workflow for running Martini based simulations.
The program was introduced for facilitating running multiscale simulations206 and was

                                                                                           94
later used as basis for running high-throughput simulations with the DAFT protocol 207. To
support deployment of the Martini 3 force field and allow integration in parameter
exploration/testing pipelines, several changes were made to the program. In particular,
the program can now make use of force field files that are provided by the user, rather
than only those distributed with the program.
       Martinate is distributed together with the atomistic counterpart gromit and can be
obtained from github (https://www.github.com/marrink-lab/gromit). The program requires
a UNIX/Linux environment with GROMACS 11,12, martinize2 and insane82. The latter is
needed for building lipid bilayers as well as for setting up PBC and generating the solvent.
The basic usage of martinate allows simple setup and running of coarse grain simulations
with the Martini force field. The current default is Martini 2.2, which is distributed with the
program. In its simplest form it is sufficient to provide the program with a PDB file. If the
PDB ID is known, it can be downloaded automatically using the -fetch option:


martinate.sh -f 1ubq.pdb -fetch pdb


       The fetch option also allows downloading membrane proteins from the OPM
database208. The martinate command-line further allows specifying the membrane to be
built by insane directly:


martinate.sh -f 2oar.pdb -fetch opm --insane-d=5 --insane-l=DPPC --
insane-sol=W


       To use martinate with martinize2, which is necessary for using the Martini 3 force
field, the martinize executable can be specified (and similarly -insane works for specifying
an alternative insane executable):


martinate.sh -f 2oar.pdb -fetch opm --insane-d=5 --insane-l=DPPC --
insane-sol=W -martinize /path/to/martinize2

       As the Martini 3 force field is currently distributed separately, using it with martinate
additionally requires specifying the location of the force field files and the version, like


martinate.sh -f 2oar.pdb -fetch opm --insane-d=5 --insane-l=DPPC --
insane-sol=W -martinize /path/to/martinize2 -ffdir /path/to/martini3 -
cg martini304

simulation system. The Martini 3 force field comes with possibilities for amending
topologies, which are currently not supported by martinize2, but which require post-hoc
processing. Although this cannot be directly incorporated into the martinate workflow, the
program does support stopping the protocol at a specific step and continue from that step
at a later time. This feature was added to support running part of the workflow locally and
finalize on a different machine (cluster), but can be used to make changes to the structure

Epilogue


Recipe of the official Martini 3 cocktail
by Pim W.J.M. Frederix


 1. Prepare a cucumber syrup by pureeing a cucumber
 and adding half-3/4 of its weight in sugar, depending on
 your sweet tooth. Dissolve and homogenize by boiling
 briefly.

 2. Add 0.5 units of Martini extra dry vermouth and 2
 units of good quality vodka to a cocktail shaker with ice
 cubes. Shake briefly but vigorously.

 3. Add 0.5-1 tablespoon of cucumber syrup to the
 bottom of a Martini glass.

 4. Use a bar spoon (or teaspoon) to carefully pour the
 Martini mixture on top of the cucumber liquid. While
 phase separation will occur spontaneously (try a
 concentrated sugar solution and an alcohol solution
 using the force field), it is easier to keep the phases
 separated to begin with. The ice should remain in the
 shaker.

 5. Serve with a cocktail pick or rosemary twig decorated
 with a cucumber, olive and caper bead and watch the
 Regular, Small and Tiny beads partition over the two
 phases.
